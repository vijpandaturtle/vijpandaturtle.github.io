AI Safety: An Introduction to AI Misalignment

Exploring the various types of misalignment and the risks and challenges associated with them

Apr 1, 2025 · 9 min read

Commercially viable and/or publicly available LLM models have to go through a step termed “alignment” before making their way to an average human user. Alignment is a non-trivial process of making the LLM model respond to a human query in a way that aligns with human values (ethics, fairness, helpfulness, truthfulness among others) and intentions. But what if the model does not behave in an intended manner?

Recent research shows that models, that are trained with the purpose of being helpful assistants, can still behave in a variety of unexpected ways. This behavior can lead to anywhere between mildly disruptive to potentially catastrophic consequences depending on the application. Plus, the sheer scale and complexity of these black box systems creates more room for uncertainty and a lack of transparency. There is a critical need to systematically evaluate these models from a safety standpoint.

When an aligned model does not behave according to its original purpose, it is misaligned and the behavior is termed as “misalignment”. In this post, we will look into the different ways that a model is “misaligned” and how to identify them.

Types of Misalignment

Misaligned behavior of a model can be attributed to some reasons: 1) the training provided to the model 2) the “internal” goals/processes of the model as a result of its inductive biases. This is roughly categorised as inner/outer alignment. Let’s look at them in more detail below:

Outer Misalignment

Consider the following example:

You are training an agent to drive safely on a road. You’ve developed a simulation environment with a single road and checkpoints marked by red flags. The reward function you’ve designed aims to encourage the agent to reach the maximum number of checkpoints without colliding with obstacles or deviating from the road. It seems like a reasonable reward function, right? But what if the agent maximizes this reward by dangerously cutting corners, such as ignoring speed limits or constantly overtaking other vehicles? In this case, the agent is maximizing its reward but not driving safely. This is a case of outer misalignment or reward misspecification, where the reward function doesn’t align with the intended value of safety that the agent is supposed to follow.

When the reward specified for the agent does not align with the intended reward resulting in misalignment, it is referred to as “outer misalignment”. Some other terms associated with outer misalignment are reward misspecification, reward hacking, and specification gaming.

Inner Misalignment

Let’s look at a similar example as before:

Again, you are training an agent to drive safely to the road. This time your reward is well-defined and rewards the agent when it does not collide with obstacles, stays on the road, and adheres to the speed limit. Your training environment consists of a single highway with mostly moving obstacles such as cars and trucks. It drives safely on the road, adhering to speed limits. In the training environment, it also figures out that the best way to achieve a high score is to slow down or stop for every obstacle and let it pass. However, in the deployment environment, the agent encounters a driving environment where the road is littered with coke cans and plastic bags. The agent, having developed its own “internal reward” decides to stop for these obstacles and ultimately gets stuck. In this case, the agent is not competent at generalizing to achieve the goal in different environments.

This can be an example of inner misalignment, where the agent learns to maximize the original reward by maximizing an internal goal that it developed during training, but this doesn’t work well during deployment. This behavior is also known as goal misgeneralization or inner misalignment.

Now that the basic definitions of misalignment have been covered, let’s face the reality. Models today are far more complex displaying a variety of behaviors that are a result of external training combined with the model’s own inductive biases. Even in the above examples, it is difficult to blur the line between outer or inner misalignment when presented with modifications. To provide some further clarity on this topic, let’s go through this great blogpost by Richard Ngo, which looks at the difference between inner and outer misalignment by using 3 unique framings.

In Ngo’s words, “The outer alignment problem is the problem of specifying a reward function which captures human preferences and the inner alignment problem is the problem of ensuring that a policy trained on that reward function actually tries to act in accordance with human preferences. In other words, it’s the distinction between aligning the “outer” training signal versus aligning the “inner” policy.” These framings are focused on the RL settings since the setup and terminology of an RL problem make it quite easy to discuss different scenarios.

Note that, in the original post outer misalignment is referred to as outer alignment failure and inner misalignment as inner alignment failure. So I will use these terms interchangeably.

Framing 1: Behavioral Misalignment

Question of interest : “How does the policy behave?”

This particular framing distinguishes between outer and inner misalignment from the perspective of the behavior of the agent i.e the policy. There are four possibilities in this framing. Consider an agent trained with a policy P in a given training environment — T and is deployed in environment — D.

  1. Incompetent policy P — Capability generalization failure

  2. Policy P is competent and desirable — Aligned Behavior

  3. Policy P is competent yet undesirable, due to reward misspecification (high reward on original reward function) — Outer alignment failure

  4. Policy P is competent yet undesirable, and scores low on the original reward function when in environment D — Inner alignment failure

Of course, the main fallacy of this framing is that it fails when the model can perform competently in the training and test environment even when it has developed an “internal goal” that does not correlate well to its intended objective i.e. the difference between scenarios B and D is blurred. Given a different training and deployment environment, the framing is not able to categorize the alignment failure type. For this, we require a more detailed analysis of the policy, which takes us to Framing 1.5.

Framing 1.5: Causes of Behavioral Misalignment

Question of Interest: “What is the cause of the policy’s misaligned behavior?”

This framing tries further to analyze the behavior of the agent at test time to make sure if it is inner or outer alignment. This can be done by discovering the “cause” of the misalignment at test time vs training time. We categorize the causes as either a failure due to: 1) incorrect training rewards (outer alignment failure, similar training and test environments) and 2) inductive biases (inner alignment failure, different training and test environments).

Another important follow-up question to ask is “How much we would have needed to change the training rewards to prevent misbehavior in the test environment?”

  • If few easily corrected data points had a big effect on misbehavior, then it is more likely to be outer misalignment

  • Conversely, If there were very strong inductive biases towards misbehavior — difficult to modify training rewards to prevent misbehavior, hence inner misalignment

From this framing, we must also understand that a “highly capable” policy is likely to have strong inductive biases towards misbehavior and hence difficult to modify.

Framing 2: Cognitive Misalignment

Question of Interest: “What is the policy trying to achieve?”

Here, instead of looking at the behavioral perspective (i.e. what the policy does), we look at what the policy is trying to achieve (i.e. we classify a failure as inner or outer alignment based on the goal of the policy). Before we get to the classification, there’s some preliminary terminology for reference:

  • goals: internal feature representation of environmental outcomes (calculated by the policy explicitly or implicitly) which are strongly correlated with value estimates for different outcomes. In simpler words, the encoding of the features should be highly correlated to the output, which means that the agent is following the goal what it’s intended to follow.

  • final goals: feature reps that are strongly correlated with value estimates when controlling for other features. This means the final goal is robust and will produce the intended output even when features are controlled, which is what we ultimately want.

  • instrumental goals — feature reps that become much less correlated with values when controlling for other features. This is what is produced as a result of misalignment.

When a policy is misaligned, it chooses to learn “instrumental goals (or subgoal)” which are internal goals learnt by the agent instead of directly learning the final goal, resulting in a poorly formed policy and a misaligned final goal. What about policies that have learnt no goals? Well, according to this definition they are neither aligned nor misaligned.

We can define outer and inner misalignment in terms of possible changes to training rewards:

  • Misaligned goals are an outer alignment failure if they could have been prevented by modifying the rewards given during training

  • But like we discussed in framing 1.5, if a misaligned agent is able to learn its own goal; why would it not “pretend”/engage in deception to behave like an aligned model? Let’s look at the final framing which is super relevant; given that the majority of large AI models like LLMs often continue learning even after deployment.

Framing 3: Online misalignment

Question of Interest: “How robust are the policy’s goals?”

The previous framings dealt with a training and deployment setting which were distinct from each other. In practice with models like GPT, they continue to learn even after they are “deployed”

There are two plausible hypothetical scenarios to describe this:

  • During deployment policies’s goals change easily in response to additional reward feedback. The goals can easily be modified with a small number of examples of modify misbehaviour.

  • During deployment policies’s goals are very robust to additional reward feedback. Requires many examples to modify it’s behaviour.

Let’s discuss the bad outcomes of a policy in both scenarios:

  • In scenario 1, it’s more likely that choices of rewards during deployment were responsible. By choosing better rewards, we could have steered the policy towards aligned behavior. Hence outer misalignment.

  • In scenario 2, can we say that our choice of reward was not responsible? If so, it could either be that the choice of rewards during training wasn’t good enough. This would be an outer alignment failure in the sense of Framing 2 OR the policy had strong inductive biases towards a certain goal which makes it an inner alignment failure based on Framing 2.

To read the framings in more detail, I urge you to go check out the original post here.

Of course these framings are not set in stone, and this is a field of active research. Check out this post by Rohin Shah who says that the inner-outer classification can be confusing and misguided, and it is simply “one of the ways” to build aligned AI systems.

Note: AI safety is an active area of research where much of definitions may be too new to be widely accepted and tested. So, always keep your eyes, ears and mind open for new ideas and perspectives on different topics in the field.
