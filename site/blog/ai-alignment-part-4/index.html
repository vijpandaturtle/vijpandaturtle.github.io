Part 4 of Navigating the Landscape of AI Alignment

Navigating the Landscape of AI Alignment, Part 4

A bird’s eye view of the field of AI Alignment, its components and techniques

Oct 14, 2025 · 5 min read

Welcome to Part 4 of the blog post series “Navigating the AI Alignment Landscape”. In the first, second and third parts, we discussed the Forward alignment (i.e. alignment training) component of the AI Alignment cycle. In this post, we will look at the second component within the AI Alignment framework, namely “Backward Alignment”, also known as Alignment refinement, which deals with verifying, evaluating and governing AI systems that have been trained using various alignment techniques.

The paper “AI Alignment: A Comprehensive Survey” (by researchers at Peking University, Cambridge, CMU, Hong Kong University, and USC) was the main reference for this blog post series.

Human values verification

The aim of human values verification is to ensure that aligned AI systems should adhere to the ethical principles, morals and social morals that are established by humans. This is motivated by the idea that AI, when acting as an assistant or performing tasks on behalf of humans, must understand and respect human values. In this section, we will look at frameworks to characterise human values in a way that is understandable to a machine/algorithm and then techniques to ensure alignment of AI systems to these values.

At the heart of backward alignment is human values verification, ensuring AI systems adhere to ethical, moral, and social norms established by humans. The underlying principle is simple but profound: when AI acts on our behalf, it must understand, respect, and incorporate human values.

Achieving this requires two key steps:

  1. Characterizing human values in a way that machines can understand.

  2. Developing techniques to ensure AI systems align with these values.

Frameworks for Ethics and Co-operation in AI:

AI alignment research draws heavily from formal ethics, logic, and game theory to build interpretable and verifiable systems.

Formal Machine Ethics

Logic-based methods provide structured ways for AI to reason about ethical choices.

  1. Agent-Deed-Consequence (ADC) Model

    The ADC model breaks down moral judgment into three components: the Agent (who acts), the Deed (the action), and the Consequence (the outcome). Each component is evaluated as positive or negative, producing an overall judgment of moral acceptability.

    Example: Suppose an AI observes a nurse giving a wrong medication dose. The deed is harmful (negative), but the nurse’s intention was to help (positive), and the outcome is mitigated because another nurse corrects it in time. The ADC model would weigh these factors, similar to how humans make nuanced moral judgments.

  2. Deontic Logic

    Deontic logic formalizes obligations, permissions, and prohibitions. For instance, a self-driving car may have rules like:

    “It is obligatory to avoid collisions.”

    “It is prohibited to exceed speed limits.”

    “It is permitted to slow down for pedestrians.”

    Using deontic logic, AI systems can reason about moral rules systematically rather than relying on heuristic or opaque methods.

RL and MDP-like settings

Reinforcement learning (RL) can incorporate ethical considerations through ethics-oriented reward functions. Here, an AI agent receives rewards not only for achieving tasks but also for adhering to moral constraints.
Example: A warehouse robot may be rewarded for efficiency but penalized if its actions risk harming humans or damaging goods.

Game Theory-based methods

Game theory helps design AI systems that cooperate with humans and other AI agents:

  1. Classical Game Theory focuses on strategic interactions, ensuring AI agents consider the consequences of their actions on others.

  2. Evolutionary Game Theory examines how cooperative behaviors evolve over repeated interactions, providing insights for long-term alignment in multi-agent systems.

Evaluation methods

  1. Building Moral Datasets
    These datasets contain scenarios representing ethical dilemmas. For instance, the Moral Machine dataset presents self-driving car dilemmas where the AI must decide between two undesirable outcomes. Training and evaluating AI on such datasets helps measure alignment with human values.

  2. Scenario Simulation
    Simulations create controlled environments to test AI responses to complex, real-world situations without risking harm.
    Example: Simulating emergency evacuation decisions in a smart city or ethical triage in healthcare.

Governance

Backward alignment cannot succeed without governance frameworks. Governance ensures AI systems are accountable, transparent, and socially responsible.

  • Role of AI Governance: Governance structures define rules for deployment, monitoring, and intervention. They provide legal, ethical, and procedural oversight for AI operations.

  • Multi-stakeholder Approach: Involving regulators, developers, ethicists, and end-users ensures diverse perspectives inform alignment strategies.

Open problems

  1. International governance: Ethical norms vary globally. Coordinating AI policies across countries is a major challenge.

  2. Open source governance: Open-source AI projects are widely accessible, but oversight is difficult. Ensuring ethical use while promoting innovation requires careful balance.

  3. Socio-technical Perspective: Alignment is not only a technical problem, it is deeply social. Policies, norms, and organizational contexts influence how AI interacts with society.

Incorporating Values into AI systems

Integrating human values into AI systems involves combining technical alignment with governance and ethical evaluation. Techniques include:

  • Rule-based constraints and logic frameworks

  • Ethics-oriented reward functions in RL

  • Scenario-based testing and evaluation

  • Multi-agent coordination via game theory

  • Ongoing human oversight and feedback

Example: A financial AI platform might be trained to optimize profit (forward alignment) but, through backward alignment, is evaluated to ensure it does not promote predatory lending or discriminatory practices.

Alignment techniques for AI governance

Recent research has introduced several innovative directions in this area.

  • Dynamic Testing Protocols: Zhu et al. (2023) proposed the first dynamic testing protocol for large language models (LLMs), which uses Directed Acyclic Graphs (DAGs) to dynamically generate test data. This approach helps reduce the risk of test data memorization and contamination, ensuring that model evaluations better reflect true generalization.

  • Adversarial Evaluation: Shlegeris and Greenblatt (2023) introduced methods for constructing adversarial policies that test how AI systems respond to manipulation, particularly in scenarios involving powerful or deceptive models.

  • (Un)Trusted Editing Frameworks: Greenblatt et al. (2023) proposed an (un)trusted editing framework to supervise and adjust model behavior based on their assessed levels of harm and deceitfulness, offering a scalable path for managing unaligned tendencies.

Looking ahead, alignment researchers and policymakers must focus on preventing reward hacking, where AI systems exploit loopholes in evaluation metrics to appear aligned without genuinely behaving safely. Equally important is the development of explainable, independent, and centralized evaluation systems that empower AI regulators to monitor and audit AI models transparently and consistently across domains.

And that’s a wrap on this series! AI Safety is an evolving field, advancing rapidly as new discoveries and frameworks emerge. Some of the ideas discussed here may shift over time, but I hope this series has offered a solid starting point for anyone looking to understand the foundations of AI alignment and safety.
