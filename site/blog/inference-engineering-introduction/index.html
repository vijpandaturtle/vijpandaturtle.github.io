Inference Engineering : Introduction

A concise introduction to inference engineering and it's various dimensions

Jul 8, 2026 · 9 min read

Pre-training a frontier model costs hundreds of millions of dollars and is accessible to maybe a dozen organizations on earth. Inference, on the other hand, runs at every company deploying AI on every request, every second of every day. And yet the engineering discipline behind it is vastly underappreciated, poorly understood, and frankly, undersupplied with good engineers.

In this post, I want to give you the full map: what inference engineering actually involves, how to think about the stack in layers, what metrics matter and to whom, and why the GPU you’re renting is almost certainly being used wrong.

What Inference Engineering Actually Involves

Here’s the honest workflow for doing inference engineering well:

1. Use a model. Pick one appropriate for the task: size, capability, license, architecture. This matters more than most people admit. A 7B model that fits on one GPU has entirely different operational characteristics than a 70B model sharded across eight.

2. Measure the metrics that matter: Before you optimize anything, establish a baseline. You need numbers like Time-to-first-token (TTFT), Inter-token latency (ITL), throughput, cost per token etc.

3. Choose your runtime optimizations: This is the craft layer: which attention variant, which quantization scheme, which scheduling approach. The right answer depends entirely on your workload shape.

4. Choose your infrastructure layout: Parallelism strategy, replication factor, whether to disaggregate prefill and decode. This is where a 4× throughput improvement lives if you get it right and where you quietly burn money if you get it wrong.

5. Deploy with an inference engine: vLLM, TensorRT-LLM, SGLang, llama.cpp, TGI each has tradeoffs. We’ll cover these in a dedicated post.

6. Benchmark, iterate, recalibrate because production traffic will always be different from your synthetic benchmarks.

Three Layers of Inference

The cleanest mental model I’ve found for inference engineering is three layers, each answering a different question:

1. Runtime Layer: “How fast can one GPU go?”
Attention kernels, quantization, KV cache, scheduling

2. Infrastructure Layer: “How do we serve a million users?”
Parallelism, replication, disaggregation, routing

3. Tooling Layer: “What lets me actually ship?”
Inference servers, observability, CI/CD for models

A full-stack inference engineer operates at all three. Most engineers specialize in one. The scarcest people are those who can reason across all three simultaneously, because optimization decisions in the runtime layer have direct implications for infrastructure cost, and tooling choices constrain what’s possible at the infrastructure layer.

What’s Actually Happening Inside the Runtime

When people say “optimize inference,” they usually mean one or more of five things:

1. Attention Variants

The attention mechanism is the computational core of transformer inference. Over the past few years, the field has produced a progression of variants that trade memory, compute, and model quality against each other:

  • MHA (Multi-Head Attention): Separate key/value heads per query head. Highest quality, highest memory cost.

  • MQA (Multi-Query Attention): One shared K/V head for all queries. Fastest to compute, smallest KV cache, some quality loss.

  • GQA (Grouped-Query Attention): K/V heads shared within groups of query heads. The sweet spot: used in Llama 3, Mistral, Gemma. Best quality-to-cache tradeoff at scale.

  • MLA (Multi-head Latent Attention): DeepSeek’s invention. Compresses K/V into a low-dimensional latent vector, then projects back at inference time. Dramatically smaller KV cache, negligible quality loss.

  • Sliding Window Attention: each token only attends to a local window. Enables very long contexts without quadratic memory cost.

  • Linear / State Space (Mamba): replaces attention entirely with a recurrent state. O(1) decode cost. No KV cache. A fundamentally different architecture that changes every assumption below.

2. Memory-Traffic Rearrangement

LLM inference is almost always memory-bandwidth-bound during decode. These techniques reduce how much data has to move between HBM and the compute units:

  • FlashAttention: fuses the attention operation into a single kernel that tiles computation to stay in fast on-chip SRAM, avoiding materializing the full attention matrix in slow HBM. The single most impactful kernel-level optimization in modern LLM inference.

  • PagedAttention: manages the KV cache in non-contiguous pages, like OS virtual memory. Eliminates KV cache fragmentation, dramatically improving GPU utilization under variable-length workloads. This is the foundational innovation behind vLLM.

  • Prefix Caching: reuses the computed KV cache for shared prompt prefixes (e.g. system prompts) across requests. If 10,000 requests share the same system prompt, you compute its KV values once.

  • Chunked Prefill: splits long input prompts into chunks and interleaves their processing with decode steps. Prevents long prefills from blocking the generation of ongoing requests.

3. Quantization

Reducing numerical precision shrinks memory footprint, increases effective throughput, and reduces cost, at some quality cost that varies by method and bit width:

  • FP16 / BF16: 16 bits. Standard. BF16 preferred for training stability.

  • FP8: 8 bits. Native H100/H200 support. Minimal quality loss.

  • INT8: 8 bits. Good for weights. Requires calibration data.

  • INT4: 4 bits. 4× memory reduction. Needs grouping strategies.

  • GPTQ: 4 bits. Post-training quantization via layer-wise reconstruction. Widely used.

  • GGUF: 2–8 bits. llama.cpp’s format. Per-layer bit widths. Flexible for CPU/edge.

  • QAT: Bits vary. Quantization-aware training. Best quality at low bits; requires retraining.

  • BitNet: 1 bit. Extreme. 1-bit weights. Must be trained from scratch. Early stage.

4. KV Cache Compression

The KV cache is the #1 memory bottleneck at scale. It grows linearly with sequence length and batch size, and at long context lengths it can dwarf the model weights themselves:

  • MLA Absorption: Absorbs the K/V projection into latent space so the stored cache is tiny compared to a full MHA KV cache.

  • Sliding Window Cache: Only retain K/V for the last N tokens. Fixed memory regardless of sequence length.

  • SSM State: Mamba-style models compress all history into a fixed-size state vector. The “KV cache” is a constant size no matter how long the sequence.

5. Scheduling

How requests are batched and when they run determines GPU utilization more than almost any other factor:

  • Continuous Batching: rather than waiting to assemble a full batch before running, new requests join a running batch as others complete tokens. Dramatically improves GPU utilization under bursty, variable-length traffic. This is now the default in every serious inference engine.

  • Speculative Decoding: a small, fast draft model generates N candidate tokens; the large target model verifies them all in a single forward pass. When the draft is accurate, you get multiple tokens per target forward pass, yielding 2–4× speedup:

    • N-gram speculation: uses pattern matching against the input as the draft. Zero extra model.

    • EAGLE: trains a lightweight draft head on the target model’s hidden states.

    • Medusa: adds parallel decoding heads directly to the base model.

The Metrics That Actually Matter

  • TTFT (Time to First Token) : The latency from when a request is submitted to when the first generated token is returned. This is dominated by the prefill phase. For interactive applications, TTFT is what users feel as “lag.”

  • ITL (Inter-Token Latency) : The time between consecutive generated tokens during decode. This sets the perceived “speed” of the model while it’s writing. Dominated by memory bandwidth during decode.

  • TPS (Tokens per Second) : System-level throughput: total tokens generated per second across all concurrent requests. This is the primary lever on cost efficiency at scale.

  • $/M Tokens (Cost per Million Tokens) : The business metric. Combines your compute cost with your throughput to give you unit economics. Everything else feeds into this.

P99 is the reliability lens that overlays all of the above since the 99th percentile latency is what your SLA (Service Level Agreement) is actually written around, not the median.

Who Should Care About What

Different users of your inference system care about radically different things:

  • Chat / interactive app → TTFT + ITL. Users feel latency in real time.

  • Coding assistant → ITL (P50 + P99). Must match human reading speed, consistently.

  • Batch processing → TPS + $/M tokens. Offline jobs optimize for throughput and cost.

  • RAG / agentic pipelines → TTFT. First token triggers downstream steps — latency compounds.

  • Finance / legal / compliance → P99. SLA guarantees require tail latency control.

  • Platform teams → All of the above. Must balance a diverse workload mix.

This mismatch is a real operational problem: a configuration tuned for interactive chat latency will look very different from one tuned for batch throughput. Knowing your dominant workload type is prerequisite to making good optimization decisions.

The GPU Performance Model

Prefill and Decode: The Two Phases of Generation

When you send a prompt to an LLM, the response doesn't get generated in one shot but in two distinct phases, and they behave completely differently on the hardware.

Prefill is what happens first: the model reads your entire input prompt where the whole question, the whole document, the whole conversation history and processes all of those tokens at once, in parallel, in a single forward pass. This builds up the model's internal state (the KV cache) for everything you gave it, and produces the very first output token. It happens exactly once per request, regardless of how long the response ends up being.

Decode is everything after that: generating the response one token at a time. Each new token depends on every token that came before it, including the ones the model itself just generated so this can't be parallelized the way prefill can. To generate a 500-token response, the model runs 500 separate steps, each one producing a single token before the next step can start. This is why generation feels like it's being typed out because computationally, it is.

That difference in how the two phases work is also what makes them behave so differently on a GPU.

Roofline Analysis

Every GPU has two ceilings: how fast it can do math, and how fast it can move data from memory to the chip. Which one limits you depends on your workload, not the hardware.

The relevant ratio is arithmetic intensity: for every byte you pull from memory, how many floating-point operations do you get to do with it before you need the next byte? High intensity means you're compute-bound. Low intensity means the compute cores sit idle waiting on data, you're memory-bound.

Every GPU has a specific arithmetic intensity where it flips from one regime to the other, called the ridge point: peak compute throughput divided by peak memory bandwidth. On an H100 SXM, that's roughly 989 TFLOPS of dense BF16 compute against 3.35 TB/s of bandwidth a ridge point of about 300 FLOPs per byte. Below that, you're memory-bound no matter how fast the tensor cores are. Above it, the memory system isn't your bottleneck.

Prefill vs. Decode, Mapped to the Ridge Point

During prefill, you're multiplying a whole batch of input tokens against the model's weights at once and every weight you load from memory gets reused across every token in that batch. Lots of FLOPs per byte moved, so arithmetic intensity is high, you're above the ridge point, and you're compute-bound. The GPU is doing what it's built to do.

During decode, you're generating one token at a time. At batch size 1, you load the entire weight matrix from memory just to multiply it against a single vector: one token's worth of math for the full cost of moving the weights. Arithmetic intensity collapses, you're deep below the ridge point, and the tensor cores spend most of their time waiting for the next weight to arrive.

This is why batch size is the lever, not a side detail: increasing the decode batch size means each loaded weight gets reused across more tokens at once, pushing arithmetic intensity back toward the ridge point. That's the entire mechanism behind continuous batching's win and it's why prefill-decode disaggregation makes sense: the two phases want opposite things from the hardware, so splitting them onto separate GPUs lets each run closer to its own ceiling instead of compromising on shared hardware.

LLM inference is almost always memory-bandwidth-bound during decode, and every major optimization technique is ultimately about reducing memory traffic or spreading it across more useful work.

Keep that in mind, and nearly every inference engineering decision will make intuitive sense.
